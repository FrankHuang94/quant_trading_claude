    Myths vs reality¶

    TL;DR

    • 28 beliefs about quant trading that turn up in pitch decks and press coverage, each checked against evidence. More than 20 are about strategies or algorithms.
    • Most myths take a true mechanism and drop one ingredient: the costs, the number of trials, the tail, the counterparty or the capacity. Ask for the missing ingredient.
    • Backtests shrink live, published anomalies lose about half their return, and ML gains are measured in tenths of a percent of R². None of that makes quant fake; it means the edge is small, costly and crowded.
    • Some popular "myths" are open debates (PFOF, 0DTE, value). They get a short answer here and both sides in Contested debates.

    Level: Core · Read time: ~13 min · Last verified: 2026-11

    Each entry gives the myth, the reality in two to four sentences with a confidence tag, and the page that explains the mechanism. Tags: [Verified] primary source, [Reported] credible secondary source, [Estimate] our arithmetic, [Lore] widely repeated but unverified.

    Research, backtests and signals¶

    1. "A high backtest Sharpe means the strategy works"¶

    A backtest shown after many tries is a maximum, and maxima of noise are large. Five years of daily data pin an annual Sharpe ratio down only to about ±0.45, so the best of 100 zero-skill strategies shows about 1.1 by luck [Estimate]. Across 888 Quantopian algorithms, backtest Sharpe explained under 2.5% of the variation in out-of-sample Sharpe [Reported] (Wiecki et al. 2016). Ask for the trial count and the deflated Sharpe ratio. See overfitting and multiple testing.

    2. "A t-statistic above 2 proves the signal is real"¶

    The 1.96 hurdle assumes one test. After hundreds of published factors, Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016) argue a new one needs t > 3.0 [Verified]; Harvey and Liu's haircut turns a Sharpe of 1.0 over 120 months, picked from 100 tests, into 0.44 [Verified]. Overlapping return windows inflate t-statistics further unless corrected (Newey–West). See evaluating signals.

    3. "Published anomalies keep paying"¶

    Across 97 predictors, returns were 26% lower out of sample and 58% lower after publication (McLean and Pontiff 2016) [Verified]. Part is statistical bias in the original papers, part is readers trading the signal away. Haircut academic gross returns by a third to a half before costs [Estimate]. See alpha decay detection.

    4. "Higher accuracy or higher IC means more profit"¶

    Profit is hit rate times payoff minus costs, and turnover sets the costs. A signal with an IC of 0.05 at 80% daily turnover can lose to one with 0.02 at 5% (illustrative). Medallion is reported to be right on only about 50.75% of its trades [Reported] (Zuckerman 2019); breadth and sizing do the rest. See cross-sectional alpha.

    5. "More features and more data always help"¶

    Every feature is another chance to fit noise and another path for look-ahead leakage. In Gu, Kelly and Xiu's 94-characteristic study, a few families (momentum, liquidity, volatility) carried most of the signal for every model [Verified]. Practitioners report that most alternative datasets they trial add nothing after costs [Lore]. See feature engineering and alternative data.

    Models: ML, LLMs, optimizers and sizing¶

    6. "ML and LLMs make return prediction easy"¶

    In the best-known study, trees and neural nets explained 0.33–0.40% of monthly stock-return variance out of sample, against 0.16% for a three-variable regression [Verified] (Gu, Kelly and Xiu 2020): twice as good, still tiny. LLM headline scores predict next-day returns, but those returns fall as LLM adoption rises [Verified] (Lopez-Lira and Tang 2026). A model pretrained on later text has also read the future in any earlier backtest. See LLMs and foundation models.

    7. "Reinforcement learning learns to trade end to end"¶

    Published RL results mostly beat simple baselines, not production algorithms; the best-documented production use is bank execution, such as J.P. Morgan's LOXM (2017) [Reported]. Simulators cannot show how the market reacts to you, and there is only one history to learn from. RL as a hedge fund's profit engine is [Lore]. See RL in trading.

    8. "The optimizer finds the optimal portfolio"¶

    Mean-variance optimization multiplies an inverted, noisy covariance matrix by noisy forecasts, so it bets hardest where estimates are most wrong; Michaud (1989) called it error maximization [Verified]. With 500 stocks and 252 days the sample covariance cannot even be inverted (Lab 14: rank 251). Shrinkage, factor risk models, constraints and cost penalties make it usable. See MVO and covariance shrinkage.

    9. "VWAP is best execution"¶

    VWAP is a benchmark, and an easy one, because your own trades sit inside the average. In Lab 17 a VWAP order that cost 12.8 bps against the arrival price looked like 8.2 bps against interval VWAP (synthetic lab result); real orders of 10% of daily volume cost nearer 20 bps [Estimate]. Implementation shortfall against arrival measures what the decision cost. See VWAP, TWAP and POV and TCA.

    10. "Full Kelly is the optimal bet size"¶

    The Kelly criterion maximizes long-run growth only if you know the true Sharpe; at twice true Kelly leverage, expected growth falls to zero. Sharpe estimates are noisy, so practitioners run a quarter to a half Kelly or less [Lore]. See position sizing.

    11. "Quant firms have one secret formula"¶

    A firm runs a pipeline of dozens of algorithms, from point-in-time data to quoting and hedging, and the edge usually sits in the joints: forecast meets cost model, fair value meets inventory. Even Medallion, described as one unified model, is reported to combine many weak signals [Reported] (Zuckerman 2019). See there is no single algorithm.

    Strategies¶

    12. "Stat arb and market-neutral books are riskless"¶

    Statistical arbitrage has positive expectation and fat-tailed crowding risk. Khandani and Lo's simple contrarian strategy had decayed so far that matching its 1998 return took about 9:1 leverage, and it lost 6.85% unlevered in three days of August 2007 [Verified]. Market neutrality removes beta, not the risk that everyone holds the same positions. See stat arb overview.

    13. "Factor investing is free money"¶

    Classic long-short factors earned gross Sharpe ratios of about 0.3–0.7 over their full histories [Verified] (our computation, French and AQR data), with long drawdowns: HML value lost 51% from January 2017 to September 2020, then gained 68% to December 2022 [Verified]. The premium is the pay for sitting through that. See factor investing fundamentals and value, quality, low vol.

    14. "Trend following is dead"¶

    It was said after weak years such as 2016 and 2023 (−4.2%). In between, the SG Trend Index returned +27.4% in 2022, and it was up 15.8% in 2026 through September [Reported]. The payoff is lumpy by design, and live returns trail backtests: 5.75% a year since 2000, maximum drawdown 20.6% [Reported]. See trend following and CTAs.

    15. "Selling options is steady income"¶

    It is selling insurance: small, frequent premiums and rare, large claims. The variance risk premium is real; the VIX averaged 19.4 in 1990–2026 against 15.3 for the volatility subsequently realized [Verified] (our computation). But on 2018-02-05 the VIX went from 17.31 to 37.32 in one day [Verified], and delta hedging removes direction, not gaps. See volatility risk premium.

    16. "Index inclusion is a free 5–8% pop"¶

    The S&P 500 inclusion effect was 7.4% in the 1990s and under 1% in the 2010s (Greenwood and Sammon 2025) [Verified]. Announcements are public and widely traded; what remains is mostly pay for supplying liquidity to index funds at the close. See flow-driven strategies.

    17. "Highly correlated stocks make a good pair"¶

    Correlated returns do not tie prices together; cointegration does, and testing for it at scale manufactures false pairs. In Lab 08 about 49 of 1,719 same-sector pairs passed p < 0.05 each period, and only about 3 of those were real (synthetic lab result). See pairs trading.

    18. "Renaissance proves markets are easy to beat with math"¶

    Medallion reportedly averaged about 66% a year gross and 39% net of fees in 1988–2018 [Reported] (Zuckerman; not an audited series). It is capped in size and run for insiders. In 2020 Renaissance's funds for outside investors lost (RIEF −19%, RIDA −32%) while Medallion gained 76% [Reported]. One capacity-capped book says little about what large pools of capital can earn. See the Renaissance profile.

    19. "AI will replace quants"¶

    LLMs already write much research code and turn text into features, and firms are scaling compute: XTX Markets says it runs more than 25,000 GPUs [Reported]. But public models read public text, so their signals commoditize; Lopez-Lira and Tang find returns to ChatGPT headline signals fall as adoption rises [Verified]. The scarce inputs remain proprietary data, cost and risk models, and judgment about which result to distrust [Lore]. See NLP and LLM signals.

    Market making, HFT and market structure¶

    20. "HFT is front-running"¶

    Front-running means trading ahead of a customer order you hold, which FINRA Rule 5320 bars [Verified]. HFT market makers trade against public orders and are themselves adversely selected: Menkveld's (2013) HFT earned €1.55 per trade on the spread and lost €0.68 on positions [Verified]. The sharper criticism is different: HFTs detect large institutional orders and end up trading alongside the informed ones (van Kervel and Menkveld 2019) [Verified]. See equity market making.

    21. "Market makers bet on direction"¶

    They earn the spread and shed inventory within seconds to minutes. In Menkveld's data, positions held under five seconds made €0.45 and longer ones lost €1.13 [Verified]. An options market maker delta-hedges and is paid for flow and for realized versus implied volatility. See quoting and inventory control and options market making.

    22. "Market makers must hold prices up in a crash"¶

    Since December 2010, registered equity market makers must quote within 8%, 20% or 30% of the NBBO, depending on stock and time of day [Verified]. That bans penny stub quotes; it does not oblige anyone to absorb a one-sided wave of selling. See algorithmic risk controls.

    23. "PFOF means retail gets worse prices"¶

    Contested. One Rule 605 study estimates wholesaler price improvement saves retail investors close to $1B a month versus exchange execution [Verified] (Dyhrberg, Shkilko and Werner, working-paper version). Yet in 85,000 simultaneous orders, round-trip costs ranged from 0.07% to 0.46% across six accounts, and payment levels did not explain the gap [Verified] (Schwarz et al. 2025). See retail flow internalization and the PFOF debate.

    24. "HFT caused the Flash Crash"¶

    The CFTC-SEC report traced the trigger to a 75,000-contract E-mini sell program (about $4.1B) executed at 9% of volume without regard to price or time [Verified]. HFTs kept trading as prices fell but did not absorb the imbalance; in 14 seconds they traded over 27,000 contracts, mostly among themselves, for about 200 net [Verified]. See the failure gallery.

    25. "Speed matters for every quant strategy"¶

    Speed is the edge only where the signal decays in microseconds to seconds: market making, latency arbitrage, lead-lag. A mid-frequency book's alpha decays over days, so trading cost binds, not latency; for earnings drift, waiting a day can beat trading into event-day spreads. See how infrastructure constrains algorithms.

    26. "Latency arbitrage is a huge hidden tax"¶

    It is real but small per trade: about 0.5 bp of trading volume in FTSE 100 stocks, roughly $5B a year across global equities (Aquilina, Budish and O'Neill 2022) [Verified]. The cost falls first on liquidity providers, who pass it on in spreads; removing races would cut the cost of liquidity by about 17% [Verified]. Whether that justifies redesigning markets is debated. See latency arbitrage.

    27. "Dark pools are unregulated"¶

    Most are alternative trading systems: broker-dealer venues under Regulation ATS that must publish Form ATS-N disclosures on order types, segmentation and operator conflicts [Verified]. Off-exchange trading was 50.6% of US equity volume in 2025 [Verified], but ATSs were only about 19% of it; wholesalers trading as principal were most of the rest [Reported]. See venues and retail flow.

    28. "Cancelling most of your orders is spoofing"¶

    Market makers cancel most orders because quotes must follow fair value. US commodities law defines spoofing as bidding or offering with intent to cancel before execution [Verified]; the 2015 CFTC case against Navinder Sarao turned on layered orders he allegedly never meant to fill [Reported]. Intent, not the cancel rate, is the line. See fee- and microstructure-driven strategies and rules that shape strategies.
