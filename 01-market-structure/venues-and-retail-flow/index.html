    Venues and retail flow¶

    TL;DR

    • US stocks trade on 18 exchanges (as of October 2026), 33 NMS-stock ATSs and over 200 other off-exchange dealers. In 2025 off-exchange volume was 50.6% of the total, the first annual majority, and about four-fifths of it went through principal dealers such as wholesalers.
    • Wholesalers pay retail brokers for marketable orders (PFOF) because retail flow is "less toxic": it rarely predicts the next price move. They can give price improvement and still earn a spread.
    • Flow toxicity is measured by markouts: how far the price moves against the liquidity provider after the fill.
    • Of the SEC's four December 2022 proposals, two were adopted (Rule 605 disclosure; ticks, access fees and round lots) and two were withdrawn in June 2025 (the Order Competition Rule and Regulation Best Execution).

    Level: Core · Read time: ~6 min · Last verified: 2026-11

    Wholesalers and other dealers 220+ other FINRA members traded off-exchange in Q1 2023 [Verified] Principal trading against client flow: retail wholesalers, single-dealer platforms, block desks 81.3% of TRF volume, about 41% of the total [Estimate] (0.813 × 50.6%)

    Off-exchange (TRF) share rose 361 bps in 2025 to 50.6% [Verified], so 2024 was about 47.0% [Estimate]. Liquidnet reports some swing back toward lit venues in 2026 on its own measure [Reported]. TODO(verify): 2026 year-to-date TRF share once full-year data are out.

    The market is this fragmented because Regulation NMS (2005) obliges every venue to respect the best protected quote anywhere. That makes a new exchange instantly reachable, and fee competition does the rest (see fees, rebates and ticks).

    Wholesalers and payment for order flow¶

    When the SEC proposed its Order Competition Rule in December 2022, it found that brokers sent more than 90% of individual investors' marketable orders to six wholesalers [Verified]. Academic work using 2019–2022 data finds that two of them, Citadel Securities and Virtu, handled over 70% of US retail flow [Reported]. Wholesalers fill those orders as principal at or better than the NBBO, usually with price improvement, and pay the broker PFOF. Robinhood's 2025 Rule 606 reports show payments from tens of cents to about a dollar per 100 shares, depending on wholesaler, order type and stock group [Reported]. Citadel Securities paid about $341M for US retail equity and options flow in Q2 2025 alone [Reported].

    A wholesaler's per-share income statement for a 100-share retail buy (illustrative: $50 stock, NBBO $49.99 × $50.01):

    Line ¢ per share
    Sell at $50.006 (0.4¢ price improvement); spread captured vs mid $50.00 +0.60
    Adverse selection: mid drifts 0.10¢ up over the next minute −0.10
    PFOF to the broker −0.15
    Hedging, exchange fees, clearing, technology −0.10
    Net +0.25

    Run the same fill against an informed taker whose one-minute markout is 1.5¢ and the result is −1.15¢. The whole business rests on being able to tell those two counterparties apart.

    Flow toxicity: why retail is "less toxic"¶

    Flow toxicity is the probability-weighted damage a counterparty does to a liquidity provider. It is the expected price move against you after you trade with them. Glosten and Milgrom (1985) showed that a dealer facing some informed traders must widen the spread to everyone. VPIN (Easley, López de Prado and O'Hara, 2012) estimates toxicity from volume imbalance. Practitioners mostly measure it directly with markouts. The realized spread is the effective spread minus the subsequent price move.

    Retail marketable orders are small, uncorrelated across accounts and rarely timed on information that matters over the next seconds to minutes, so their markouts are small. The key difference is segmentation. A wholesaler knows which broker sent the order, so it knows the flow is retail. An exchange sees anonymous orders and must price for the average counterparty. The two sides of the debate:

    • For the current model: retail gets better-than-NBBO fills and zero commissions, paid for by the low cost of serving uninformed flow.
    • Against: the SEC estimated a $1.5B-a-year "competitive shortfall" versus order-by-order auctions [Verified]. Critics also argue that taking benign flow off exchanges leaves lit quotes facing more toxic flow, so lit spreads are wider for everyone.

    The evidence is mixed. This KB takes no side.

    Disclosure: Rules 605 and 606¶

    • Rule 605 requires monthly execution-quality statistics: effective spreads, price improvement and speed. The March 6, 2024 amendments extend it to brokers with 100,000 or more customer accounts, add a summary report, and add realized spreads at several horizons from 50 ms to 5 minutes [Reported]. Compliance moved from December 14, 2025 to August 1, 2026 [Verified]. Short-horizon realized spreads are, in effect, standardized public markouts.
    • Rule 606 requires quarterly broker routing reports: where orders went and the PFOF received per 100 shares.

    Why this matters for strategies & algorithms¶

    • Retail flow is a strategy. Retail flow internalization is the wholesaler business above. Its risk model is adverse selection and markouts, and its pricing engine is fair value and microprice.
    • Toxicity sets the quote. Equity market making and quoting and inventory control widen or skew quotes on toxicity estimates. Latency arbitrage is the most toxic flow there is.
    • Venue choice is an algorithm. Smart order routing ranks venues by fill rate and post-fill markout. Auction, close and dark-liquidity algos handle the dark pools. Transaction cost analysis audits the result.
