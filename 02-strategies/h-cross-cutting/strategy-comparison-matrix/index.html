    Strategy comparison matrix¶

    TL;DR

    • One row per dossier, condensed from its header table, with the confidence tags carried over. Treat every number as an order of magnitude and click through for the reasoning.
    • Capacity and Sharpe trade off. Market-making and speed books show Sharpe ratios above 3 where the number means anything, on profit pools of a few $B a year for the whole industry; risk-premia books run tens of billions at net Sharpe 0.3–0.7.
    • Evidence quality runs the other way: slow strategies' Sharpe ratios are [Verified] from decades of public data, fast strategies' are mostly [Lore].
    • Strategies cluster by failure mode: crowded deleveraging (stat arb, mid-frequency alpha, factors), short convexity (volatility selling, dispersion, carry, options market making), and technology or rule changes (speed and market making). Diversify across clusters, not across rows.

    Level: Core · Read time: ~11 min · Last verified: 2026-11

    Every dossier opens with the same header table, so the 30 strategies compare directly. This page lines them up. Cells are compressed to a few words; the dossier holds the period, source type and reasoning behind each figure.

    How to read this table¶

    Capacity comes in three units, and they do not compare. For portfolio strategies (families C–G) it is the gross market value or notional one firm can run before costs eat the edge. For market making and speed (A–B) capital is not what binds; the flow available to intermediate is, so the dossiers quote an annual profit pool, usually for the whole industry. Dispersion quotes vega. "$1–3B a year of P&L" and "$1–10B of GMV" describe different things.

    The Sharpe column mixes sources on purpose. An arrow separates gross from net when the dossier gives both; otherwise the cell says which. Academic gross backtests are upper bounds, measured over different decades, and published anomalies lose about 58% of their in-sample return after publication (McLean and Pontiff 2016) [Verified]. For a market maker, Sharpe ratio on capital is "n/m": the capital at risk is small, and technology, data and staff costs, not volatility, decide whether the business pays.

    Turnover times cost is the drag. A book trading 50× its GMV a year at 5 bps per dollar traded pays 2.5% of GMV a year before any alpha (illustrative). That is why the short-horizon rows live or die by their transaction cost models.

    Positions are approximate and illustrative, read off the table above. Market-making Sharpe is measured on the small capital those books use.

    The upper-right quadrant is nearly empty. The reason is market impact: under the square-root law, cost per dollar traded rises with the square root of trade size, so total cost grows like size^1.5 while gross alpha grows like size. Profit peaks at 4/9 of the break-even size (derivation in the reversal dossier). Fast strategies hit that wall early because they trade their book 50–250 times a year; factor and trend books trade it a few times a year and run tens of billions.

    2. Short horizons show higher Sharpe¶

    The fundamental law of active management says the information ratio is roughly skill per bet times the square root of independent bets per year, the breadth (Grinold 1989) [Verified]. Illustrative: a daily residual book with an IC of 0.01 and ~200 independent bets a day has breadth of about 50,000, so IR ≈ 0.01 × 224 ≈ 2.2. A monthly factor book with IC 0.05 but only ~20 independent bets a month has breadth 240 and IR ≈ 0.8. A market maker's day is thousands of nearly independent small bets, which is how Virtu's IPO filing could show one losing day in 1,238 [Reported]. The catch is the previous section: breadth bought with turnover runs into capacity.

    3. The best numbers have the weakest evidence¶

    The [Verified] Sharpe ratios cluster in families E–G, computed from public data spanning four decades or more. Nearly every market-making and speed figure is [Lore] or a single academic case study, because those firms do not publish strategy returns. The most reliable numbers in the table are the lowest ones.

    Trigger → strategies that lose together → dated episode. Grouping drawn from each dossier's failure-mode field.

    "High" crowding appears in most rows; what matters is who is crowded with whom. In August 2007 stat-arb and factor books lost together, consistent with a rapid unwind of overlapping positions (Khandani and Lo 2011) [Verified]. The short-convexity rows lose in the same volatility spike. The speed rows fail one firm at a time, through code or lost latency rank, or all at once through rule changes such as the half-penny tick and 10-mil access-fee cap, now delayed to November 2027 [Verified]. Episodes are in the failure gallery.

    5. Complements for a multi-strategy book¶

    Rows from different clusters diversify each other; rows from the same cluster do not. Trend following had about zero correlation to US equities over 1880–2013 and made money in 8 of the 10 worst 60/40 drawdowns (Hurst, Ooi and Pedersen) [Verified], the opposite profile to volatility selling and carry. Value and momentum correlated −0.19 month to month over 1963–2026 [Verified] (our computation, French data). Short-term reversal's expected return rises with the VIX (Nagel 2012) [Verified], but only after the drawdown: in March 2020 the daily factor lost 18.4% before gaining 31.7% [Verified]. Capacity complements too: small, high-Sharpe books add return per unit of risk, while large, low-Sharpe books absorb capital. How firms combine strategies gives the arithmetic, including the \(S/\sqrt{\rho}\) ceiling that crowding imposes.
