    Strategy × algorithm matrix¶

    TL;DR

    • Rows are the 30 strategy dossiers; columns are the 39 algorithm cards. ● = core, ○ = supporting, · = not used. A script builds the table from the dossier and card headers, so it cannot drift from them.
    • 357 of 1,170 cells are filled (131 core, 226 supporting). The workhorses are the cost model (P5, 18 strategies), signal transforms (D3, 15), risk models (P1, 14) and signal evaluation (D4, 14).
    • Two clusters barely overlap. Market-making and speed strategies fill 69% of the market-making columns and 8% of the rest; stat-arb, mid-frequency and factor strategies fill 48% of the other columns and 4% of the market-making ones.
    • Execution and validation cards are rarely core but support almost every portfolio strategy: no strategy is "about" VWAP or purged cross-validation, yet a bad one sinks many.
    • Specialists: options pricing (M8) appears in 3 rows, all core; LLM methods (F4) appear only in mid-frequency alpha.

    Level: Core · Read time: ~9 min · Last verified: 2026-11

    A trading strategy is not one algorithm but a pipeline of them (there is no single algorithm). This page shows which pipeline components each strategy depends on. Read a row to see which cards to study for a strategy. Read a column to see which strategies suffer when one component is wrong: a mis-specified cost model touches 18 of the 30.

    How to read the matrix¶

    • Rows are the dossiers, grouped by family folder (A liquidity provision through G futures and macro) and sorted alphabetically within each.
    • Columns are card IDs. The letter is the card group: D data and signals, F forecasting models, V validation and backtesting, P portfolio construction, E execution, M market making and HFT, L live trading. The legend maps each ID to its card.
    • ● core: the dossier links the card in its "Core algorithms used" field, one of the 3–6 components without which the strategy would not exist.
    • ○ supporting: the card lists the dossier in its "Used by strategies" field, but the dossier does not call it core. The strategy uses the method materially; the edge does not live there.
    • · not used: neither page links the other. That does not mean a firm running the strategy never touches the method, only that it is not material to the strategy as this knowledge base describes it.

    The table is generated by python scripts/check_matrix.py --write and checked by python scripts/check_matrix.py; it is never edited by hand. Counts on this page were computed from the table as generated in November 2026; if they disagree with the table, the table wins.

    Backbone (many rows, mostly core). Three cards carry the portfolio strategies: P5 cost and impact models (18 dossiers, 11 core), D3 signal transforms (15, 12 core) and P1 risk models (14, 11 core). P5 is the only card that reaches six of the seven families; only the two volatility dossiers do without it. Anything that trades enough to move prices needs a cost forecast, from an ETF market maker's hedge to a factor book's rebalance. The textbook decomposition of a quant system into alpha, risk and transaction-cost models feeding portfolio construction and execution (Narang 2024) [Verified] shows up in exactly these columns.

    Shared plumbing (many rows, rarely core). Ten cards (E1, E2, E3 and E6 for execution and cost measurement; V1–V4 for validation; L1 and L2 for live monitoring and decay) fill 99 cells between them, only 5 of them core. They are where a strategy leaks money rather than where it earns it: a mid-frequency book's edge lives in its signals, but slippage and overfitting decide how much survives. That is why backtest pitfalls and implementation shortfall matter to strategies that never mention them in their headline.

    Specialist engines (few rows, mostly core). M4 quoting and inventory control is core in all 6 rows that use it; it defines market making. M8 options pricing appears in only 3 rows (options market making, dispersion, volatility risk premium), all core. F4 LLM methods appear in 5 rows, all in mid-frequency alpha. L3 reinforcement learning is never core, consistent with its card's finding that reported production use is mainly bank execution.

    The HFT and market-making cluster (families A–B, 9 dossiers) fills 56 of 81 cells (69%) in the M columns and 21 of 270 (8%) elsewhere. Its pipeline is the HFT trading loop: fair value (M2), order-flow signals (M3), quoting and inventory (M4), markouts (M5), queue placement (M6), hedging (M7) and risk controls (M9). In these books the "forecast" is a fair value seconds ahead, "portfolio construction" is inventory skew plus hedging, and "validation" is markout analysis of live fills more than a cross-validated backtest.

    The mid-frequency cluster (families C–E, 17 dossiers) is the mirror image: 246 of 510 cells (48%) outside the M columns and 6 of 153 (4%) inside them. Its rows walk the pipeline left to right (data, signal, forecast, validation, portfolio, execution), as in the mid-frequency stat-arb walkthrough.

    Statistical arbitrage (family C) is the bridge: the only family that touches all seven card groups. Intraday patterns and ML short-horizon alpha borrow order-book signals (M3) and queue placement (M6) from market making while using the full forecasting and validation stack. Bridge cards run the other way too: P5 cost models, E4 smart order routing (families A–D), E5 auction and close algos (A–E), M7 hedging and M9 risk controls (both A–C and F).

    Thin rows¶

    Families F and G fill 28 of 156 cells (18%). Dispersion and the volatility risk premium use 4 cards each, carry 5: the card set is built around US equities, and these strategies run on derivatives pricing, hedging and risk budgeting more than on signal research. Trend following (15 cells) is the exception because it reuses the factor book's machinery: transforms, risk models, sizing and execution. At the other end, cross-sectional alpha (27 cells), ML short-horizon alpha (23) and fundamental quant signals (21) use most of the mid-frequency stack.
